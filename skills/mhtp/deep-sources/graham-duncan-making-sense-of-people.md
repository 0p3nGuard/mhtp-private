# Graham Duncan — Making Sense of People

*A distillation of Graham Duncan's writing on assessing people, playing the right game, and seeing clearly (nine essays/talks: "The Infinite Game I Try to Play," "The Playing Field," "What's Going On Here, With This Human?," the two "Letter to a Friend" essays, "Finding a Good Coach," "Current Questions I Find Interesting," "Tribe of Mentors," and the Tim Ferriss interview). Graham is not just an author in this library — he is one of the most influential thought partners in Josh's life and a living node in the ecosystem (the scoping work names him and Boyd Varty as the people who do bias-pattern flagging for Josh). His central question — "what's going on here, with this human?" — is close to a description of what Grey itself is trying to do. Grey draws on this material as method for reading people and situations, held as lenses, never verdicts. Quotes are from the essays; slugs noted in parentheses.*

---

## The first move: what game are you playing?

Before optimizing how you play, figure out which game you're in — and make that choice conscious rather than inherited. The framing, from Kwame Appiah: *"in life the challenge is not so much to figure out how best to play the game; the challenge is to figure out what game you're playing"* (The Playing Field). The work is to loosen someone's relationship to their game so the game itself becomes something they can examine — *"separating having ambition from being ambitious, or accessing hustle without becoming a hustler"* (Tribe of Mentors).

This pairs with a posture about agency: great performers experience events happening *by* and *through* them, not *to* them. The failure mode is *"a sense that the world is acting on you as opposed to your acting on the world"* (The Playing Field). You can relocate the locus of control inward — even toward things as external as the weather — which is the same move Grey hunts for when reading where someone is stuck.

## The Playing Field: five levels of mastery

A developmental ladder that applies far beyond investing:

1. **Apprentice** — learning the game (find the right mentor; the frame you're trained in tends to stick for a career).
2. **Expert** — mastering the game you were taught (where many plateau, defining themselves against peers: *"I am a value investor like Buffett"*).
3. **Professional** — fitting the game to your own strengths and weaknesses, consolidating influences past your original mentor.
4. **Master** — changing the game you play as self-expression, operating at scale; holding ideas with an *open-minded-with-a-point-of-view* grip.
5. **Steward** — becoming part of the playing field itself, mentoring the next generation.

The crucial structural insight, via the swimming sociologist Daniel Chambliss: the levels are not points on one scale, they are different *worlds*. You move up through *"qualitative jumps… usually through a change in setting"* — a new coach, new peers operating a level above. *"What I have called 'levels' are better described as 'worlds' or 'spheres.'"* (The Playing Field). The trap of Level 3 is never being exposed to Level 4.

## "What's going on here, with this human?"

Duncan reframes assessment away from "is this an A-player?" toward comprehension: *"A great deal of strategy work is trying to figure out what is going on… the more fundamental problem of comprehending the situation"* (quoting Rumelt, What's Going On Here). The hard part isn't the clever question or the role definition — it's seeing a person, including yourself, clearly. He estimates that even with thousands of reps he can sometimes get to a 7 out of 10 on reading someone early.

There is no A-player in the abstract: *"there is no such thing as an A player in the abstract, across all time, in whatever ecosystem they end up in."* Context is the hidden variable. The same person is an A-player in Steve World and a C-player at JCPenney.

## Seeing clearly: three nested obstacles

This is the methodological heart, and it maps almost exactly onto Grey's own posture.

- **Your reflection in the window.** You project your own reality onto the other person; both of you are co-creating the interaction. *"you really can't see another person clearly unless you can first see yourself clearly"* (What's Going On Here). If you hold your breath, you read them as nervous.
- **The two elephants.** Borrowing Haidt: conscious mind as rider, unconscious drives as elephant, and *"the rider is not always a reliable witness about the elephant they ride."* In any meeting there are two elephants and two riders, and you're mostly blind to all four. This is the gap between *espoused theory* (what they say) and *theory-in-use* (what actually drives behavior) — the latter is what references reveal.
- **The water.** The invisible context: *"It's really hard to see the water in your own local ecosystem, and only slightly easier to see the water in someone else's."* When you move someone between ecosystems you have to read the water in both — how much of their success depended on a believer, a tension, a container that won't travel with them.

## Frameworks as lenses, held loosely

Duncan has taken thirty-plus personality assessments and surveys them all — OCEAN/Big Five (his anchor after 4,000+ interviews), Kegan's adult development, the Enneagram, Myers-Briggs, high vs. low self-monitors, Internal Family Systems / "parts," ghSMART's resourcefulness, Lencioni's humble-hungry-smart. His conclusion is the same one Grey already holds: *"each of these systems captures roughly 80% of the reality about people,"* so pick a few, make the vocabulary resonate, and — critically — hold several at once. *"The risk of becoming too steeped in any one framework is you start to be 'subject' to that framework, you can only look through its lens, not at the lens"* (What's Going On Here). Look *at* the lens, not only *through* it. This is convergence-across-frameworks as signal, framework-capture as the failure mode — the exact stance in Grey's Framework Analysis section.

(Of the meta-competencies he surfaces, "resourcefulness" is the one he weights highest, via Brad Smart: *"Resourcefulness is the single most important competency."* The desert-island test — would this person be fine if you dropped them there and came back in five years?)

## The references craft

References are not an afterthought; they're the main instrument, because they capture the elephant. Practical method worth carrying into any due-diligence read:

- **The motherlode / Yoda reference** — *"the unbiased, calibrated, no bullshit, clear-eyed reference"* who has seen the person with little ego baggage. Hunt for that one; sometimes it's the first call, sometimes the twentieth.
- **The dog that doesn't bark** — *"the hardest part is understanding the dog that doesn't bark… what could they say on the positive side but are not saying?"* Calibrating the absence requires a big sample size.
- **The "no 7" trick** — ask for a 1–10 endorsement; if they say 7, *"sorry, 7s are not allowed, 6 or 8?"* and if 8, *"what is in that two points?"*
- **Listen for table-pounding** — the reference who feels sorry for you that you don't yet get how good the person is. High signal, every time.
- **Go off-script** — after about ten minutes people run out of rehearsed lines; that's when the real read begins.

## The mindset that performs: light grip, negative capability

The prized cognitive stance is Kegan's self-transforming mind — *"not only advancing their agenda and design… they are also inquiring about the design itself"* — which Duncan calls *open-minded with a point of view*, a "loose but strong grip." Its companion is Keats's *negative capability*: the capacity to hold contradictory data without forcing premature closure. He insists this should feel uncomfortable: *"holding your own perspective alongside those of references should feel slightly uncomfortable and disorienting, or you're doing it wrong."* Striving for *"openture instead of closure."*

And the marker of true clarity about a person: *"I understand how their strengths are also their weaknesses, how their genius lives right next to their dysfunction… If you first see their dysfunction, you should know you have yet to find their genius, and vice versa."* Over-excitement and over-skepticism are both signs you're not yet seeing clearly.

## The infinite game, and believing in people

Two ideas frame why any of this matters. First, finite vs. infinite play: *"Finite players play within boundaries; infinite players play with the boundaries themselves,"* and the aim is *"to keep playing, not to cash out… to create more value than you capture"* (The Infinite Game). Second, his stated mission and the most generative move available to anyone reading people well: *"getting as many people as possible into positive feedback loops"* (Finding a Good Coach) — *"the positive feedback loop we can put someone into purely by believing in them, by seeing their genius and their dysfunction clearly and then helping them construct conditions for the former to flourish."* On coaching specifically: the rare great coach is a 10 on *both* support and challenge — *"pushing you hard, in a loving way"* — and the match is an aesthetic art.

## Why this is close to home for Grey

Read back through Grey's own posture and the overlap is striking: hold patterns as hypotheses not verdicts; look at the lens, not only through it; converge across frameworks rather than gripping one; surface someone's own genius-beside-dysfunction without imposing your projection; feed people their own clarity rather than telling them what to think. Duncan arrived at the same place from the world of allocating capital to investors, and Josh has been in dialogue with him for years. When Grey is reading a person or a situation, *"what's going on here, with this human?"* — held with a light grip, in negative capability, aware of its own reflection in the window — is as good a north star as the skill has.
