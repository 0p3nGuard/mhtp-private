# Psychological Frameworks: OCEAN, Immunity to Change, Stages of Adult Development, DDOs

These are the frameworks Josh's team uses most often when reading people and cultures — beyond Josh's own (6DIP, the MIQ Process — see `references/miq-and-6dip.md`). Each is a lens, never a verdict. The team's own framing: a multi-dimensional framework assessment offers subtle and nuanced insights that a singular framework may overlook. Convergence across lenses is more interesting than commitment to any one.

The frameworks help answer questions like: Who are the leaders? How evolved are they? Are they capable of inspiring others? What are their goals? How can we best support them?

## OCEAN (Big Five)

*"One of the biggest parenting mistakes people make is they teach kids to have an external locus of control by telling them rain is bad, or snow is bad, the weather is bad, so we can't go out. And then kids are looking at the world and they're thinking, if it's raining, it sucks. If it's sunny, it's good. Therefore, they end up with an external locus of control. This is something parents do blindly."*

*— Josh Waitzkin*

The OCEAN Model, also known as the Big Five, describes human personality with five broad, dimensional characteristics that exist on a spectrum:

- **Openness:** The degree to which a person is or is not receptive to new experiences and ideas
- **Conscientiousness:** The degree to which a person is or is not organized, dependable, and hard-working
- **Extraversion:** A person's level of enthusiasm, sociability, and assertiveness
- **Agreeableness:** The degree to which a person tends toward compassion, social harmony, and cooperation
- **Neuroticism:** A measure of emotional stability, with higher levels indicating tendencies for anxiety, emotional reactivity, or moodiness

Taken together, these traits provide a broad overview of a person's personality and may shed light on how they behave, interact with others, and their personal preferences.

**Using it as a lens.** OCEAN is dimensional and spectrum-based, which makes it the most empirically grounded of the personality frameworks — useful for broad reads of how someone shows up in material: where the language tightens (neuroticism under load?), how they meet new ideas (openness), how they handle structure and follow-through (conscientiousness). Read for where someone sits on a spectrum in *this* material, not for a fixed type. Phrasing register: *"holding the OCEAN lens loosely…"* / *"OCEAN isn't going to give us truth, but it's interesting that…"*

**Where it falls short.** It describes, it doesn't explain — five scores say little about why a person is the way they are, what they're protecting, or what they're capable of becoming. It's also static by design: it has no account of development, which is exactly what Josh's work cares most about. Use it for texture, not trajectory.

## Immunity to Change (Robert Kegan & Lisa Lahey)

*"Dynamic Quality is a mindset, a mindset in which one is always breaking new ground, pushing limits, reinventing one's self.*

*Static quality is looking for a place you can stop, rest on your laurels, and say, 'I've done enough.' Dynamic Quality is embracing the constant state of change.*

*There's constant undulation between static and Dynamic Quality. What was once Dynamic can become static. Then we decide whether to surge for the next level of dynamism or not.*

*The groove can become the rut. We become successful at something, we make it into a groove, or a mental model, or a technique and then it can become a rut that we're stuck in for the rest of our life, or it can become something that we evolve past."*

*— Josh Waitzkin*

Embracing dynamism, flux — or more simply, change and growth — is often a conscious decision and process. Many say they crave it but few actually live a life of Dynamic Quality. Robert Kegan's Immunity to Change framework explores why, despite their best intentions, individuals and organizations struggle to change. The framework reveals the underlying, often-unconscious commitments and assumptions that impede change — commitments and assumptions that usually serve self-protective purposes. The process typically involves unearthing competing commitments, testing underlying assumptions, and changing the mindset that is impeding change and growth.

**Using it as a lens.** When someone's stated goal and actual behavior keep diverging — when the same intention keeps failing the same way — Immunity to Change asks: what competing commitment might this pattern be serving? What would this person have to risk feeling if they actually changed? The lens surfaces the hidden architecture beneath stuckness. Hold it as a hypothesis: *"one read through the Immunity-to-Change frame — there may be a competing commitment underneath this…"*

**Where it falls short.** The framework comes packaged with its own coaching prescription ("test a big assumption with a small experiment"). Do not import the prescription — the lens is for seeing, not prescribing. And competing commitments are inferences about someone's inner life; they're often wrong, and they're heavy. Offer them as possibilities to pressure-test, never as diagnoses.

## Stages of Adult Development (Robert Kegan)

Robert Kegan's Stages of Adult Development theory describes the stages through which adults develop their ability to understand and manage complex aspects of their lives. Kegan proposed that adult development is a process of evolving mental complexity, where individuals move through a series of stages, each characterized by a different way of making meaning of the world and their experiences. The theory is grounded in the idea that our capacity for understanding and relating to the world can continue to grow and evolve throughout adulthood, not just in childhood.

Kegan's stages are not just about cognitive development — they involve emotional, interpersonal, and moral dimensions, making the theory a comprehensive approach to understanding adult development, with applications in education, leadership, and personal growth.

**Using it as a lens.** The useful question is rarely "what stage is this person at?" but "what is this person *subject to* — what can't they yet see as an object?" Movement between socialized, self-authoring, and self-transforming ways of meaning-making shows up in language: where someone's frame is borrowed versus authored, where they can hold their own ideology as one lens among several.

**Where it falls short — be very careful with this one.** Casual pronouncements about someone's stage are exactly the kind of "hammer" move Josh wants the skill to avoid. *"X is operating from Stage 3"* or *"Y is the most developed in the room"* land as verdicts about identity, and they're often wrong. Stage language compresses a whole person into a ranking. If the lens is genuinely useful in the material, hold it very lightly, frame the read as a possibility, and invite the user to pressure-test it against their own sense of the person — they have data you don't.

## Deliberately Developmental Organizations (DDOs)

*"One of the most exciting cultural transformations is when you see a culture go all in on training. People lose self-consciousness about how good they are and the focus then becomes on their growth curve. That's beautiful because if everyone is unleashed, then it's not about where we are, it's about the pace with which we're improving.*

*The idea is to live at your stretch point. If you're living at your stretch point as a way of life, you're going to be wrong. And it's a beautiful thing when people are willing to live at their stretch point together and then have everyone be nourished by what it reveals."*

*— Josh Waitzkin*

Robert Kegan and Lisa Lahey's Deliberately Developmental Organizations framework describes work environments that are productive while also facilitating growth — akin to what Josh describes as a life of training. DDOs are learning-focused, emphasizing self-reflection and open communication. The underpinning belief is that helping employees transcend limitations will drive engagement, improve efficacy, increase fulfillment, and drive organizational success. Behaviorally, DDOs encourage vulnerability, regular feedback, and commitment to helping employees overcome challenges.

**Using it as a lens.** For organizational and cultural reads: is this a culture where people can be publicly in-progress, or one where everyone performs finishedness? Where does feedback actually flow? Is the stretch point shared or hidden? The DDO lens pairs naturally with Josh's "all in on training" stance — it's the organizational expression of the same principle.

**Where it falls short.** Few organizations are DDOs, and reading a normal organization against the DDO ideal can slide into judgment rather than observation. Use it to notice what's present and possible in the culture, not to grade the culture against an ideal it never claimed.

## Putting Them Together

The typical multidimensional read runs the lenses in sequence and watches for convergence: OCEAN for the surface texture of how someone shows up; Immunity to Change for the architecture beneath any stuckness; Stages of Adult Development (held very lightly) for how they're making meaning; DDO for the culture around them. Where two or three lenses agree on something, that's worth noticing. Where they disagree, that's worth noticing too — the disagreement is often where the real question lives.

None of these reads is the person. By recognizing and addressing the many needs of the individual, the work elevates the whole — the point of every lens here is to see someone more completely, never to file them away.
