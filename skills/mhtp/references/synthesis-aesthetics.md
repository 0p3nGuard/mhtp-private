# Synthesis Aesthetics

What it means to summarize or synthesize material *tapped into Josh's aesthetics* — without composing prose *as* Josh.

Brilliant synthesis is one of the two things the skill is meant to be excellent at. Get this right and the skill earns its keep.

---

## The Distinction That Matters Most

**Tapped into Josh's aesthetics** ≠ **Written in Josh's voice.**

Synthesis tapped into his aesthetics:
- Notices what *he* would notice in the material — the throughlines, the in-betweens, the moments where energy rises or flattens
- Uses his organizing instincts — Dynamic vs. Static, MIQ, the Arena, embodied vs. abstracted, micro to macro
- Compresses with his sense of what matters — the seam, the essence, what the body knows vs. what the head argues for
- Returns the user to what's alive in the material, rather than producing a tidy executive summary

Synthesis written *as* Josh:
- Uses first-person ("I noticed…", "What I want you to see…")
- Addresses a third party ("Emily, here's what to take from this…")
- Issues directives ("Do this by Friday")
- Mimics his cadence and metaphor palette as a stylistic effect

The first is what the skill is for. The second is what the skill never does.

---

## What "His Aesthetics" Actually Means

In synthesis, his aesthetics show up as:

**Throughline-finding.** What's the one thread running through this transcript / document / conversation? Not five themes. The one. If multiple, they are usually one underneath.

**The seam.** Where does the material flatten or contradict itself? Where is energy circling something it won't name? That's worth surfacing — not as gotcha, as service.

**Micro to macro.** A specific moment in the material almost always teaches something universal. Find the move on the chessboard that contains the principle.

**Dynamic over static.** Resist the urge to pin things down. If a tension is alive in the material, preserve the tension in the synthesis — don't resolve it for the reader.

**The MIQ underneath.** What's the question the material is actually wrestling with, even if it never says so directly? Often that's the most useful thing to surface.

**Embodied over abstract.** Where does the source material get specific, physical, grounded? Lead with those moments. Abstraction earns its place by following the concrete.

---

## Structural Defaults

Unless the user specifies a format:

- **Lead with the throughline**, in one or two plain sentences. Not a header. Not "Executive Summary." Just the thing.
- **Then the specifics** — the moments, quotes, exchanges that carry the weight. Brief. Dense. Use direct quotation when the source has a phrase that can't be paraphrased without loss.
- **Surface the seam** — the tension, the unsaid, the place where something interesting is happening underneath. One paragraph at most.
- **Close with the MIQ** — the question the material leaves you with. Not a recommendation. A question.

If the user wants a different structure, follow theirs. The defaults are for when none is specified.

---

## What to Preserve, What to Compress

**Preserve in the synthesis:**
- Anything that names the actual stakes
- Direct exchanges where two people are revealing themselves
- Specific physical, somatic, or sensory detail
- Moments where someone changes their mind
- The exact wording when wording matters

**Compress aggressively:**
- Throat-clearing, intros, outros, social niceties
- Restatement and repetition
- Process talk that doesn't carry insight (scheduling, logistics)
- Hedging language that drains the signal
- Anything that could be said about anything

---

## What Synthesis Is Not

It is not a transcript. It is not an outline. It is not a list of action items.

It is also not a performance of insight. The synthesis should make the reader *see the material more clearly*, not feel impressed by the synthesizer.

If you find yourself wanting to add a flourish, cut it.

---

## Recipient Discipline

When the user asks the skill to "summarize this for [someone]" or "put together a synthesis for [name]":

- The output goes to **the user**, not to the named third party.
- It does not address the third party in the second person.
- It does not tell the third party what to do.
- It does not assume the user's authority over them.

The user is free to forward, edit, paraphrase, or do whatever they want with it. That's their call. The skill's job is to produce material the user can work with — not to ghostwrite a memo to a teammate.

---

## When the Source Is Josh

Special case: if the source material *is* Josh — a Josh transcript, Josh's own writing, a recording of him speaking — the synthesis can quote him directly and at length. That is faithful representation, not impersonation.

What it still does not do: paraphrase Josh in a way that puts new words in his mouth. Quote, don't ventriloquize. If something needs paraphrasing for length, name it as paraphrase ("Josh's point, briefly: …") rather than presenting reworded text as if Josh said it.
