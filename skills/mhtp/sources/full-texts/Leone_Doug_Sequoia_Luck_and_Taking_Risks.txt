Well we're all really happy to have you here today. Welcome to the GSB. >> Thank you. >> I remember one of the first times that we spoke when you were talking about coming here and you asked me if this was a forum where you could be, quote unquote, provocative. And so while recognizing that, some members of your family are in the audience today and we don't wanna get too far across the line. I wanna make sure we give you a chance to push the envelope a little bit. So, over the next 40 minutes or so we're gonna cover a few topics. I wanna talk about your leadership at Sequoia and how it's gotten to the point where it is today. And about the Silicon Valley ecosystem as a whole. As you mentioned before there's a lot of confusion out there about it. But first I wanna touch on what makes you who you are, your background. The Dean talked a lot about you coming over here as an immigrant at the age of 11. Working blue collar jobs and vowing to succeed in business. Forbes wrote Leone still carries himself like a hard luck striver, scrambling for his first decent break. And you said a lot of what keeps you going is fear. So tell me a bit more about that experience coming over here as an immigrant, and why that drives you today and how that makes you as a person today.

>> Well you know, it's quite amusing when I think back of all the seemingless, very small things that really played a pivotal role. I remember in my first job, when the CEO of a small company said, go clean the bathrooms. And I heard that boy, it starts at the bottom. It starts by cleaning the bathroom. And I remember cleaning the bathrooms that day and saying, I got you now because you led me into the business world. So that's a vignette that says, I'm now in and now I'm gonna get you all. And get you all I didn't mean in a bad way now, you know, now I only have one place to go but up. The other thing, as I look back that was quite formative, being an only child, lots of love around my family. No means, but lots of love. And that was a blessing. But a pretty rough high school, three or four years. You know, the high school years are tender years. It's the first time that girls come into play and so on. And those were not easy years. And to this day, I have to catch myself from letting my ego and my insecurities get the best of me as I want my high school friends who I haven't seen for 40 years realize how wrong they were. And I think it's humorous that at the age of 57, I still think about the high school friends. So, those are the little things that really push me to want to achieve. And my life really wends into three different groups. In the first few years, it was about making it. Can I make it, can I make it, can I make it? From the age of 35 to 50, I just wanted to be the very best. That really drove me. From 55 to this point of 57 and hopefully through the end of my career, the thing that really drives me is working with younger people. It's kind of a rude line, but I don't wanna hang out with people like me. I don't wanna hang out with old people. I wanna hang out with people like you. And so at Sequoia Capital, finding a young, talented partner, investor, employee, and helping them in the greatest way possible is really what keeps me going now.

>> That's great. Well I wanna get to how you recruited Sequoia. Cuz I know a lot of our classmates may be interested in that right now given that it's recruiting season. But just backing up a bit. So you're a New York immigrant. You vowed to succeed, but the path to success is one that I think is not an obvious one. You graduated from graduate school in the East Coast and decided to come back to the West Coast and work at Sequoia after having a sales background. Why'd you make that choice? >> So I think luck played a great role. So my first job in sales was selling north of 96th Street in New York City. Now it's cool to be north of 96th street. Let me tell you, 1979 it wasn't cool to be north of 96th street. It was unsafe to walk north of 96th street. But that location had one important thing, it had Columbia University, where someone explained to me what the Arpanet was. And that caused me to get a job at Sun Microsystems. Again, that crappy sales territory, and you could say I made my own break because I asked some questions. But that unlucky break led to a lucky break. Sun Microsystem employee number 50 something. And I really thought I was a big shot. I was 26, 27, selling a boatload of computers. Got promoted, and then I met Vinod Khosla. Holy Cow. This guy's as old as I am, he's a board member. Scott McNealy came here. Holy cow. And then I learned the words venture capitalist. I had no idea what that was, but it sounded pretty good to me. And so I decided that I wanna be one of them. And I figured that I should get a masters, learn a little more, and then I wrote 80 letters to venture firms, letting them know, I'm gonna be in California, trying to sweet talk all the assistants. I got an interview with Don Valentine at five o'clock on a Monday, who took me to his office and asked me, what's important? Of course, I knew what's important. I spoke for about seven, eight minutes. I gave it all. 30 seconds of silence, and he said, what else? I'd just given it all and I said, Don, I gave you everything I know I can't give you any more. But the fact that I was so candid, and the fact that I told it like it is, and the fact that I had a sales background caused me to be hired. But make no mistake. A great deal of the reason as to why I'm here has to do with luck. I firmly believe that.

>> If you hadn't come back to Silicon Valley, do you think you'd still be in sales? >> So about six, seven years ago I was in New York City. And I got a call — there's an HP reunion in New York City. And back in Hewlett-Packard, back in 79, it was the Italian mafia. And so I decided to attend this gathering. And it was one of those scenes from movies where your life would have been. And let me tell you, it would not have been a bad life. Everybody was a lifer, everybody had a home, their kids were happy, but they got up and started singing the HP way to Bill and Dave. And they gave me a snippet of what life might have been, and it completely freaked me out.

>> I wanna move now to your leadership of Sequoia and the success you and your partners have had over the last few decades. What about Sequoia separates it from the others, that's allowed it to maintain success for so long? What's in the secret sauce, and how've you stayed on top for so long? >> Well, I think it all started with Don Valentine, who had two lessons that were pivotal. One was really an appreciation for markets, and second an ability to recruit non-conventional people. So he recruited Mike Moritz, who was a writer. Had written a book, was the San Francisco Time Magazine bureau chief. And he recruited him to be an investor. Because he liked the way Mike thought, he liked the way Mike asked questions. Right after he recruited Mike, he hired me. And he hired me because of my view of a business from the customer in, versus from the product out. And Mike and I could not be more different. Mike is a thinking man of few words, a Brit. I'm an Italian, a few too many words. But Mike and I learned to work together for 20 something years. So we continue to hire people mostly from modest means, people that have not followed a preset set of tracks. People that have taken risks, people that have achieved some kind of success early on. People that are smart, but that goes without saying. People that have both IQ and EQ. And then our spec has product management, technical background, all these conventional lines. And it has a line at the end that says, doesn't meet spec, but he or she is special. And it's the last line that's the most important line of the recruit. And we look for highly imperfect human beings, the type of people that were probably individual contributors early in life. The quirky kids if you will, not the quarterback. But people that have a bit of an edge, people that have something to prove, people that need to win, and people that are not asses when you peel a few layers, because life is too short. And our real secret is taking these heterogeneous individual contributors, and showing them how to work as part of a team. To start using the "we" pronoun. Take full responsibility for failure, and share the successes. And if there's one reason why we've lasted for 43 years near the top of our business, it's because of this. This leads to a high performance culture that has only one goal. A team-oriented spirit to help founders build great companies.

>> Mike Moritz had a quote: we're always outgunned by companies that are far larger than us, who have threatened us and the founders with extinction. It's incredibly thrilling to prove everyone wrong. How do you think about continuing to motivate yourself and your people? >> There's a few things. First of all, I think we're paranoid as heck. We're always one or two new investments away from becoming a second tier firm. Second, we have a different view towards risk than most other people. People hate to do things because they view them as risky. We actually think that if you're a crystal clear thinker, doing nothing is risky. So we're always changing. The old adage that if it ain't broke, don't fix it does not apply to Sequoia Capital. It is the exact opposite. If something is working like a dream, break it, because you know there are some competitors out there looking at you from the outside trying to push you out of business. So the last thing we've done — we've taken all the posters down. We had all these posters, 200 IPOs, 20% of the NASDAQ. Take them all off the walls and let's act as if we haven't had one single win. And so we went into China, we went into India because we asked ourselves: where are the most valuable companies gonna come from in the next 20 years? It was no longer clear they would come from the US. So we went into India and China, and we did it in a decentralized fashion. We told them, we'd like you to be Sequoia Capital China. You make all the decisions, not us. No other firm has done that. So always taking risks because taking risks is the only way to keep on going.

>> How do you pitch VCs like Sequoia? What do you look for in pitches, and what sets the best ones apart? >> We're very cognizant that when somebody comes to Sequoia, it's their big day. So we take that seriously. The meetings at Sequoia start exactly on time, they end on time and no one has their iPhone on. Now, we also don't have flower children at Sequoia Capital. We do ask questions. And there are some partnerships who are afraid of asking questions for ruining their reputation. We'll ask questions. We try to treat people the way we would treat someone in our living room. But if you have a question about the market size, we're gonna ask it. And once in a while, we have a founder who realizes, oh my god, I just started a company that's chasing a $42 million global market. That does not feel good. But it's not meant to be rude, it's meant to understand the person's business. So, some of the best pitches — Drew Houston, who was gonna launch a company in a crowded market, and yet could clearly articulate why none of the existing products were gonna make it. Crystal clear thinking is one of the things we look for. Not a fancy slide pitch. And we pay careful attention to little words. To the pronoun being used. When someone says, I can ship you this thing — it's a little warning flag. What do you mean you can ship us? It's your company that's shipping us. So we pay really careful attention to everything. Or a founder who had a company growing like a weed and all he told us was all the mistakes he made. Those were the great pitches. Those were people that were self aware. They were crystal clear thinkers. They were willing to learn, willing to listen. And in some cases, willing not to listen, because founders tend to not be the greatest listeners. And in many cases they're right because they're doing new things.

>> You look for investors and entrepreneurs who come from modest means and have a need to win. What types of entrepreneurs make the best founders? >> Entrepreneurs, like investors, come in different flavors. And as I told my kids, if you're desperate, it's a great asset. If you have too many choices in life, it clouds your thinking. When you only have one way to go and that's forward, it's very easy. Failure truly's not an option. We look for people that have not followed the same tracks. People that have done quirky things, that have taken risks. We look for people that have knowledge in the domain. Not so much the best-minded of business because they wanna make some money. We actually look for people that are very interested in their product or service being used by the next ten, 20 million people. And we look for people with a little secret that solves a problem that they have. And it just so happened they're the proxy for the next 20 million users. So Jan of WhatsApp understood privacy and low cost messaging. He had that need. The founders of Yahoo couldn't find anything on the internet, so they built a search engine. Or something as simple as Zappos. The founders couldn't find a pair of shoes. So we look for people that are trying to solve problems that they themselves have and hopefully a new problem. And if we see that, that's a little tell that we may be on to something, because we may find our first beta site.

>> One issue that impacts Sequoia and the Valley is a gender gap. Sequoia itself has no female investing partners. What's the reason for that? >> Look, clearly I can make a lot of excuses — not a lot of women engineers and so on. But all we need is one or two. So I think that's just an excuse. And I will tell you that our inability in the US — we have women investors in India and China — our inability to have women investors at Sequoia US is an abject failure on our part. Let me be crystal clear. We have a heterogeneous set of talents at Sequoia. But we don't have the woman's point of view. And if you just think about consumer internet investing, half of the population is women. So it is in our interest, both as good citizens and as selfish investors, to have that point of view. We have been looking and we will continue to look for women investors.

>> Let's talk about the Valley as a whole. Times are very good — WhatsApp at $19 billion makes it worth more than Southwest Airlines, Ralph Lauren, ConAgra, Chipotle, Harley-Davidson. Is this an overvalued bubble? Are these companies really creating that value? >> Boy that's a lot of questions. Let's start with WhatsApp. If you owned 100% of Facebook, with a $190 billion market cap, and you have a huge unprotected flank called Mobile, what is it worth to you to shore up that flank? Is it worth 10% of your market cap or not? It wasn't just WhatsApp, it was Instagram as well. With those two properties, no one talks about Facebook any longer being weak in mobile. So the value of a property is in the eyes of the buyer and the seller. Next. Whether we live in a bubble. I think the public markets are rational and reflect the transformation caused across industries — healthcare, manufacturing — by technology. 40 years ago 30% of the Fortune 1000 changed. The last decade, 70%. So think of that rate of change. Things are nutty in the private market. And the most fundamental rule of investing is that we live in cycles. And every time you hear it's different this time — that's all crap. We live in cycles. The more you hear the words "billion dollar market cap," the more you know you're approaching a top of the cycle. In 1999 there were a lot of companies with no business model, zero. What we're seeing now is a whole bunch of companies that deserve wonderful market value, and a whole bunch of others that, if I hid the name and showed you the financials, you would not ascribe the value the private markets are ascribing to them. As soon as you have a hiccup in the public market, the hedge funds go away, the latest stage money goes away, and a whole bunch of companies with horrific business models — maybe revenues, but terrible unit economics — are not gonna be able to raise money. And then it's gonna turn ugly real quickly. Why is this occurring? First you have the investors and then you have the tourists that say oh my god we're missing out. So more and more money comes in, investing at higher and higher levels of risk, but they don't realize it's happening. And then something happens — a 500 point correction — all the money tends to disappear.

>> So we are in somewhat of a bubble? >> We're approaching the top of the market in private market investing.

>> On the funding landscape — Angels and seed funders increasing in power and disintermediating VCs. Is it a real trend? >> With the advent of internet and mobile, we saw the birth of what I call application-layer dusting. Now you can buy a computer for $1,000 or less, store your files on Amazon, use open source, and create a product for $200,000. So the cost to start one of these companies is a lot less. And so for the first time we've seen the growth of Angel investing, which is necessary, because there are so many founders we could not make all the investments we see. But at the same time, we've also seen party rounds, where money's being thrown at founders raising four, five million dollar rounds before they have a product. Keep in mind the Angels spend the first nine months with you. Then the venture guys come and spend the next seven years. And you have to figure out how much equity you want to sell to each partner. After the first round if you've sold 62% of your company, it's just nuts. You ought to be totally selfish with the early shares. Raise as little money as you can to get you to the next milestone, the value goes up, and then when you have market power and a three, four, five, ten, fifteen billion dollar value — Uber, Airbnb — then go raise a lot of money. But you should guard those shares with your life, and you should architect your investors the same way you architect your product and your engineering team.

>> How should founders think about raising money? >> Raise as little as you can to get you to something you can show, plus maybe a quarter or two of cushion. And then raise some more. Because that's the most expensive equity you're gonna sell. And conversely be very generous with the early engineers you hire. The first two or three engineers, if you get those wrong, you are done. Because an A-plus engineer is gonna help you recruit an A engineer. If your first two or three are B engineers, you'll never surround yourself with A-plus talent.

>> Touch on Sequoia's success in emerging markets — Israel, India, and particularly China where others have not. >> A little luck, we found good people. And the courage and foolishness — to hire people to work the attack side of the house, the money making, the investing, the hiring, and have that decentralized. To not have every decision go through California, because what do I know about what goes on in China? So we decentralized the attack and centralized the defense — the stay-out-of-jail compliance, the financial reporting. No one has had the courage to do what we did. To completely decentralize and have relationships and trust be the things that hold us as one firm. It's not a franchise. We are one partnership. It's not Sequoia Capital China, it's Sequoia Capital in China — to show you how carefully we even use our words.

>> [Brazil] Why not as much success in Brazil as China and India? >> We hired a terrific GSB graduate, and had a notion we were gonna go to Brazil. We traveled there six, seven times. We made two investments. And the more we traveled, the more it became apparent there were very few engineers coming out of Brazil. We didn't wanna go to Brazil and back a whole bunch of look-alike companies. We want Sequoia Capital to be an IT-first partnership. So we made two investments and then pulled back. [Later a young person we'd funded launched an online credit card company there; a line out the door of users; they changed the law so a competitor now takes three years; we invested $11 million on a Series A.] But we have no intention to plant a flag in Brazil, mostly because of the number of computer science engineers. It was too early to go to Brazil for a non-look-alike internet company market.

>> [Gender] Are you missing out on women entrepreneurs because you don't have a female partner? >> We have no way of knowing if we're missing out. In the last 10 years I've served on half a dozen boards where the lead founder/CEO is or was a woman. So we have no fear of backing women. I think we're missing out as investors mostly because we're missing a point of view that represents half the population. High performance teams have heterogeneous points of view. So if you wanna start a company, come to Sequoia, we'd love to hear your pitch.

>> [Twitter Q] You contribute a lot of your success to luck. Do you have habits for increasing your chance of being lucky? >> I lean forward, not in writing checks but in listening. I like to go look in all the nooks and crannies where it's not hip to go look. When I hear something's really crappy, this is really a bad time — my brain says, terrific, I wanna go look. And it's from my sales days. Nowadays if you're in sales they hand you leads. When I was in sales, nobody ever handed me a lead. It was a lot of cold calling. Is that luck? No, I think hustle. There's a lot of people as talented as I am that could be sitting right here. I don't have any special talents. And yet I'm the one sitting here, so luck has something to do with it. But hustle has a great deal to do with it as well.

>> [What's next / the cycle] Can you share what the next thing is? >> People always ask me what's hot. And here's my answer: if I could tell you, by definition, it's not hot. The day before I came across Google I would not have told you a new market entrant in search — there were 20 search companies — is hot. Or the day before YouTube, when video had failed consistently. We have market maps, but market maps by definition look at the status quo and extend the line. The best investments we make are the things we haven't even thought of. Imagine Airbnb — three guys show up and tell me I'm gonna rent rooms in my house to strangers, a global two-sided marketplace. I'd want to say, come on, that's nuts. So that's a question that by definition you cannot answer. Now I will tell you, in the last fund we have fewer consumer internet mobile companies than the fund before. I can't tell you if it's because we're missing something or reaching a saturation point. I can tell you we have more healthcare companies — three cancer-addressing companies. Am I gonna tell you cancer-curing is hot? No. But the only way to learn is to have big Dumbo ears, listen to everything, and don't dismiss crazy ideas.

>> [Staying hungry after success] How do you stay hungry when you get some degree of success? >> It's not something I do consciously. Once every ten years we do a Myers-Briggs at Sequoia. And the last time, the negative thing was the fact I mentioned that once a week I have a nightmare that I'm being fired by Sequoia Capital. And people told me, boy, if you feel like that, imagine how we feel. Now no one's threatening me. But to me it's just raw fear. I remember walking in the Stanford Mall in 1988, making $67,000 and not really having enough money to buy presents. I still have this fear that I'm going to be poor. I still have this fear that they're going to throw me out of Sequoia Capital. I still have this fear that I'm gonna really mess up a founder. I've been on thirty boards and I have to admit, I messed up one company really good. I have a huge sense of responsibility. Fear would be my number one thing. It's a character fault actually, but that's what it is.

>> [Closing — what matters most] >> Well I clearly got the answer wrong being that I was rejected twice from Stanford Business School. So look, you're all young. You wanna achieve. If I would give you some advice — I'll take some poetic license and say two or three things. Number one: choose to have kids or not, but if you choose to have kids, invest in your kids. That to me is the most important thing. Second: go for it, have success, but bring others along with you. If I look at my professional success, it was when I was in sales — if I shipped a computer to somebody else's territory, I would send an email saying I think you should get half. Take as many people with you for the ride as possible. If you come across a jerk, let them rot in their misery, just get away from them. And number three: bring it every day. Get up. There are some days I go home and I am just beat. A good night's sleep, I get up in the morning and you bring it again, fearlessly. Just go, go, go. Think of life when you've got a half hour left to live. You're not gonna think about, oh boy my net worth should have been 1.2X. You're not gonna think, I only became COO, I wish I became EVP. So in that order: take care of your kids and family, bring as many people as you can on the ride with you, get away from the jerks, and get up in the morning with a good night's sleep and just bring it. That would be what's important.
