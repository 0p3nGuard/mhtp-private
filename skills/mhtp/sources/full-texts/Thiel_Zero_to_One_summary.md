# Zero to One — Peter Thiel (with Blake Masters), 2014
*Own-prose reference summary for MHTP (copyright-safe distillation, not verbatim). Source PDF received from Joe; held as reference. Part of Matt's canon.*

## Thesis
Progress comes in two forms. **Horizontal / "1 to n"** is copying what works — globalization, doing more of the same. **Vertical / "0 to 1"** is doing something genuinely new — technology, creating what did not exist. The book is about the second: how to build a company that creates a new thing rather than competing in an existing category. Grew out of Thiel's Stanford CS183 startup course.

## The contrarian question
The intellectual signature of the book: *"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"* Good answers are unpopular-but-true — a proprietary insight the world hasn't yet accepted. Great companies are built on such secrets.

## Competition is for losers; monopoly is the goal
Thiel inverts conventional wisdom: perfect competition destroys profits, so the aim is to **escape competition** by building a durable monopoly — a business so good at what it does that no rival can offer a close substitute. Four traits of lasting monopolies: **proprietary technology** (a rule of thumb: ~10x better than the next best), **network effects**, **economies of scale**, and **brand**. The playbook: **start small and dominate a niche**, then expand outward (Amazon: books → everything; Facebook: one campus → the world). Beware the framing traps — companies in real competition describe their market as huge (a tiny slice of a giant market) while monopolies disguise themselves by describing their market as the union of many.

## The power law
Returns are radically unequal: a single company (or investment) can outweigh all the others combined. The practical consequences — for founders and investors alike — are **focus and concentration**: do the one thing that can be enormous rather than diversifying across the merely good. "Last mover" advantage (durability of cash flows far in the future) matters more than being first.

## Secrets
A core belief: **there are still important secrets left to find** — hard truths about the world (and about people) that are neither impossible nor conventional. The contrarian temperament is the willingness to look where others assume everything is already known.

## Definite vs. indefinite optimism
Thiel prefers **definite optimism** — having a concrete plan and designing the future (the Jobs/SpaceX mode) — over **indefinite optimism** (iteration, optionality, finance-as-worldview). Great companies are conspiracies to change the world according to a plan.

## Founders and people
The **Founder's Paradox**: the individuals who create 0-to-1 companies tend to be extreme, spiky, contradictory outliers — and organizations need them even as they're hard to manage. Emphasis on the founding team, on culture, and on getting the earliest hires right. Sales and **distribution** matter far more than technical people want to admit — a superior product still needs a way to reach the world.

## The seven questions every business must answer
Engineering (breakthrough, not incremental), timing (why now), monopoly (start with a big share of a small market), people (right team), distribution (can you deliver), durability (defensible in 10–20 years), and the secret (a unique opportunity others don't see).

## Why it's in Matt's canon
Maps tightly onto how Matt thinks: the **contrarian truth / contrarian-leaf-node** instinct; **differentiation and monopoly** as the source of durable value; the **power law** behind concentration and conviction; **definite optimism / pro-growth** worldview; and the **spiky-founder** phenotype ("some dimension 100x better").
