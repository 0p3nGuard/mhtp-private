>> You've clearly had a very impressive career but before becoming one of the— >> That's not what my mother says. >> [LAUGH] Before becoming one of the top venture capitalists in the world, being knighted by the queen, you were a boy from Wales. What did you want to be when you grew up? >> Actually, not that this game or sport is played in America, so it won't translate, I wanted to be a cricket player, a cricketer. But I was a coward, and the ball was very, very hard. And so I spent most of my time in sport, whether cricket or rugby, trying to avoid being anywhere near the ball. I have a friend who became a spectacular rugby player, he played for Wales, and I told him that the only thing that separated him and me on the rugby field was when he took to the field, he wanted the ball to come in his direction. And I of course wanted the reverse, I never wanted to see it come in my direction. As I got a bit older I became interested in journalism, and when I was at Oxford I knew that I wanted to be a journalist.

>> I'm interested in how your family came to Wales. Your parents escaped Germany, arrived in Wales as refugees. How did their experience shape you? >> Well they were very lucky. They didn't escape during the second World War. They escaped beforehand. My father left Germany, he was born in Munich, and went to Britain when he was 14. My mother went on something called the Kindertransport — 10,000 kids from Germany that were rescued and put in foster homes. They met subsequently in Britain and after the war they got married. And those searing experiences — my father's father served in the German army during World War I, he was decorated, and my father's mother was a nurse in the German army during World War I, and then both of them were murdered by the Nazis. My father died about 15, 16 years ago. My mother, I saw her last week, is 95 and still vibrant. But those experiences color everything. They hang over everything like a dark cloud. It is always the anxiety and the fear of the whole world being ripped out from underneath you, and it colors the way that you live. >> And that drives you today? >> Well I don't know about that. But it certainly had a profound effect on my parents, and they came to Wales as outsiders. Britain opened its borders and allowed them to settle there. But it was not easy. They were in a minority. I was one of three Jewish kids at the high school I went to. So one always felt an outsider in the delineation between religions in Britain, which 40, 50 years ago was very sharp.

>> You worked hard to get into Oxford, had opportunities in Europe, but decided to come to the United States. Why? >> Well, I didn't have many professional opportunities in Europe because I grew up in the Britain that predated Margaret Thatcher. Those were extremely difficult times — a strike-ridden country, where you never knew whether the electricity was going to work. And I wanted to be a journalist. I wanted to work on Fleet Street, at the Times or the Daily Telegraph or the Guardian. But because of union restrictions, the big newspapers were not permitted to hire anybody straight out of undergrad. You had to serve a multiyear apprenticeship on small provincial newspapers, and I had no appetite for that. I went to see the editor of the Daily Telegraph, a Fleet Street legend, a character on whom Evelyn Waugh had based the novel Scoop. And he said, if I was your age, get out of Britain. Go to America. And that was what sent me to America — that weird, peculiar encounter. I was lucky. I wouldn't have been able to come had I not got a scholarship. I came to America in 1976 not knowing where anything would lead.

>> You started as a journalist for Time Magazine, in Detroit, covering the first collapse of Chrysler, then made your way to San Francisco to cover technology, and got to know Steve Jobs. What made Jobs such a unique innovator? >> This is 1980. I remember going to a small management event at Apple, probably 1982 or '83. And Steve had a fireside chat like this, and asked the guest — a Morgan Stanley analyst — pick the year when you think Microsoft will break the $10 million revenue barrier. So it's a very long time ago. I realized I hadn't met anyone like Steve. I'd spent time with a fellow called Lee Iacocca, a living legend, president of Ford and CEO of Chrysler. And Steve was the first person whose salesmanship was on a par with Iacocca's. But Iacocca didn't have the product and engineering attributes that Steve had. Steve was a spellbinder. And obviously, as everybody knows, a very difficult and complicated character. But I have nothing but massive admiration for what he eventually went on and did. At that time I probably held a minority opinion of how spectacular an individual he was. Later he was fired from the company he founded, which to me always seemed like a real travesty.

>> How did you make the switch from Time to venture capital by joining Sequoia? >> I'd left Time because, while I enjoyed four years as a journalist, I also got frustrated. And for some reason it always felt I didn't want to be a journalist when I was 30 years old. Largely because I saw what happened to people who stayed in journalism and were 60 years old — certainly if they worked for Time, a cushy job, but you got sent all over the world and you wound up as an alcoholic. Which appeared to me to be a really beckoning career trajectory. So I left, and together with somebody else started a little company that published newsletters and staged conferences about the technology industry. That fellow stuck with it and eventually Dow Jones bought that company. Through my work I'd met a lot of people in the venture capital business — Time had run a cover story I reported on about the venture industry, with Arthur Rock on the cover. So I made a list of five firms where I knew somebody, wrote to them — you wrote in those days, you didn't email — and got interviewed at all, got rejected at four of them. Journalist, history major, no experience working in a Silicon Valley company, therefore totally useless. Probably correct on most of those counts. And then Don Valentine, the founder of Sequoia, decided he needed to do something to change Sequoia's image with minority hiring. And so decided to hire a history major. It was a head scratcher for most of Don's peers, wondering why on Earth he was wasting his time with somebody like me. It was great good fortune. If Don had voted like the others, I don't know what I'd be doing.

>> In the era of the prized engineering degree, what value does a broad-based humanities education bring? >> I obviously have a prejudice and bias. If you're in the investing business in Silicon Valley, you cover such a wide waterfront that no matter how numerate and technical you are, you cannot be an expert in everything. So the ability — much like being a journalist — to start on an endeavor where you know nothing, gather a lot of materials and facts, distill all of those facts, and then form a cogent opinion and make a decision. It's not unlike writing a story or a history essay. What comes after — helping a little company of three people become something significant — is a very different set of skills that experience brings along. It doesn't matter whether you have a technical degree. And the ability to be a storyteller and a clear communicator — those are very helpful pursuits. At Stanford, you had 647 undergrads in 1965 who were history majors. In 2014 you had 84. So they're a threatened species around here.

>> Since 1972, Sequoia has backed companies that now have an aggregate public market value of over $3.3 trillion — larger than the GDP of the UK. What are the most important leadership traits needed to build enduring organizations? >> Being able to think and work in 20-year increments is such an advantage. It sounds a little peculiar, and you can talk in grand fashion about a 20-year plan, but then you also have to work on what this afternoon brings. Having a sense of eventually where your compass is set, and the trajectory you want to go on, and then breaking it down into all sorts of steps. Knowing that every step along the journey is going to require different people, different skills, different relationships, different virtues that you're constantly going to have to change. And reacting to changing circumstances and market conditions, but never losing sight of eventually where you want to go. It's 20 years since we invested in Google; I still own the majority of my Google shares. When somebody asks why we'd be interested in investing in their company, I'll say — because if the sun shines on us, we can be shareholders in your company 20 years from now. We invested $600,000 in Airbnb in 2008 or 2009; ten years later the company is still private. Stripe, a payment services company we invested in when it was three or four people, in 2010 — nine years later. These are very long journeys. It takes time and patience, and spectacular people, and a massive market opportunity, to build the real companies that matter.

>> You and Sir Alex [Ferguson] talk about identifying high-potential talent — the example he uses is David Beckham. What characteristics are you looking for when trying to identify high-potential talent, whether a founder or a Sequoia hire? >> The reason I approached Alex to write the book about Manchester United was that people had been asking me to write a book about Silicon Valley or Sequoia, and I had no interest in that, but I was interested in writing about enduring organizations. I picked United because it was a long way from Silicon Valley, but I knew he shared the approach we cherished: identifying people when they are young and then helping them develop. Part of it is because you're not taking a lot of risk. They knew about Beckham when he was eight years old. Now, we're not going around Stanford looking for eight-year-olds, but we do like hiring people who are young. Because even though it looks simple — today everybody is an investor — to do it well in the fashion we like takes time, patience, persistence, and a willingness to learn. And assuming people like that flourish, you have incredible loyalty, and they'll stay with you forever. Being able to grow organically like that is an enormous competitive advantage — a stable team you're always amending, always replenishing the bench, but doing it imperceptibly, so all outsiders see is the winning team on the field.

>> One characteristic you mentioned is obsession. What does obsession mean to you, and how do you know it when you see it? >> There's a gentleman called Apoorva Mehta, founder and CEO of Instacart. Before we invested, I asked him how he'd happened on grocery delivery. He said he'd toyed with a variety of other businesses and gotten down the road on several. But he realized Instacart was the one when it was the last thing he thought about when he went to sleep and the first thing he thought about when he woke up. And to me that was as good a definition of obsession as any I've heard. I'll give you another example — early 1980s, I'd been in Seattle working on a story about Microsoft, when it was still private and small. Bill Gates gave me a ride to the airport in his car. And the radio was missing — a big gaping hole in the dashboard. I said, Bill, did you get ripped off? He said no, I had it taken out. Why? Well, I drive from my home to the office, which is 7 minutes and 32 seconds. And he said if I've got the radio, I'm afraid I'll switch it on and I won't be thinking about Microsoft. That's obsession.

>> The funding environment is becoming extremely competitive. Are there too many investment dollars chasing too few investable opportunities? >> That's the line I've heard since 1986, with one or two exceptions — maybe 1991, 1992, and right after the dot-com blowup, when everybody understood capital was scarce. It's always been a competitive funding environment. How many companies get financed, and how many become tremendously important companies? If you look at the funnel, there are very, very few great companies. So everybody is madly in pursuit of those companies. Money has poured into the sector. But for us it's almost always been an environment where a really spectacular idea can raise capital, where somebody starting a company usually has choices of capital. That's the world I grew up in and I suspect will always be the world we operate in. >> So no bubble? >> Well, there are always things we'll have done that five, ten years from now we'll say, how did we ever do something as silly as that? So I'm sure we'll have egg on our face from time to time. It's just an occupational hazard.

>> What responsibility does the venture industry have to fostering diversity? >> The responsibility of firms like ours is to identify burgeoning talent — the more the merrier, irrespective of gender, background, or color. That's easy to say, and bland. Firms like ours are representative of social trends. So I dug up the data on diversity at Stanford, in majors, and it's shocking. In 1983, 22% of students who elected math as their major were women. In 2014, 16%. Computer science: 21% women in 1983, 20% in 2014. We have seven female investment partners today, close to half a dozen female CEOs we've backed. But we're a reflection of society and can only change as fast as society will change. We've funded initiatives to help women in technology. But it's imperative for the high schools and universities of America to change before we'll see real acceleration. It's a lot better today than 10 or 15 years ago, but there's tons of work for all of us to do.

>> Is Silicon Valley still the best place in the world to start a company? >> It's here and it's in China. There are trials and tribulations with both places — the cost of living, taxes, all the impediments we wish weren't here. But much as people complain about Silicon Valley, you go elsewhere around the world and they all wish they were Silicon Valley. I've spent the last three weeks travelling in China and Southeast Asia — those are vibrant places. China everybody's read about, but Southeast Asia is really pulsing with entrepreneurial activity, a big change in the last five, six years. When I started in the venture business, largely Silicon Valley and to some extent Boston had 100% market share of the most important technology companies of tomorrow. That's completely changed.

>> If you were in our shoes today, what problems or industries would you focus on — where is Sequoia going to be investing? >> Well, that's assuming we know what we're doing. You go to any investor's website and despite the window dressing they all look the same — everybody's going to invest in whatever the buzz phrases of the year are: data science, machine learning, artificial intelligence. To me, the best investments are always the ones that don't fit in a convenient bucket. Think about Airbnb — what bucket would you have put three couch surfers in, in 2008? In retrospect it looks obvious, they're upsetting the hotel industry. But at the time it was three young founders sleeping on airbeds, pigeonholed as a very niche service. I can point to any number of other investments that seem similar. Yahoo — the idea of investing in a company that gave its service away for free, in 1995, was totally foreign, came out of left field. We invested some years ago in DJI, a drone company in China — nobody imagined at the time it would do $2 billion in sales, from what seemed like a nice little consumer toy, now becoming a company of substance as it expands into industrial applications. Or, unfortunately we weren't an early investor in Uber — luxury black car executive ride sharing, is that ever going to become a business? So that's the thing to look out for. It's the unexpected.
